11.1 Comparing Three Infinitesimal Operators: Time Evolution, Translation, and Rotation

Infinitesimal time evolution and energy: energy (interactions) generates time evolution.

\[ \begin{array}{l} \hat U\left( {dt} \right) = \hat I - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }dt\\ \hat U\left( {dt} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi \left( {t + dt} \right)} \right\rangle \\ \hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \end{array} \tag{11.1}\]

Infinitesimal spatial translation and momentum: momentum generates translations.

\[ \begin{array}{l} \hat \Gamma \left( {dx} \right) = \hat I - i\frac{{\hat P}}{\hbar }dx\\ \hat \Gamma \left( {dx} \right)\left\vert x \right\rangle = \left\vert {x + dx} \right\rangle \\ \left\langle x \right\vert \hat P\left\vert \psi \right\rangle = - i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial x}}\left\langle {x} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {x \psi }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {\psi } \right\rangle \end{array} \tag{11.2}\]

Infinitesimal spatial rotation and orbital angular momentum: angular momentum generates rotations.

\[ \begin{array}{l} \hat D\left( {{\bf{z}},d\phi } \right) = \hat I - i\frac{{{{\hat L}_z}}}{\hbar }d\phi \\ \hat D\left( {{\bf{z}},d\phi } \right)\left\vert {r,\theta ,\phi } \right\rangle = \left\vert {r,\theta ,\phi + d\phi } \right\rangle \\ \left\langle {\bf{x}} \right\vert {{\hat L}_z}\left\vert \psi \right\rangle = - i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial \phi }}\left\langle {{\bf{x}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{\bf{x}} \psi }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {\psi } \right\rangle \end{array} \tag{11.3}\]

Here \(\left\vert {\bf{x}} \right\rangle \equiv \left\vert {x,y,z} \right\rangle \equiv \left\vert {r,\theta ,\phi } \right\rangle\).

The derivations are similar. Why does \(i\hbar\) have a positive sign in the energy expression, while \(i\hbar\) has a negative sign in the momentum and angular-momentum expressions?

The Schrödinger equation Equation 11.1 is independent of representation, whereas Equation 11.2 and Equation 11.3 are position-representation formulas. In Schrödinger quantum mechanics, time is a parameter but position is an observable. The Schrödinger equation follows directly from the action of time evolution on a general state vector. The spatial formulas instead follow from translation and rotation of position basis vectors. Directly evolving a state and changing its basis have opposite effects on its components, accounting for the minus sign.

11.2 Connecting Discrete and Continuous Representations

Two examples illustrate qualitatively how matrices in a discrete representation become operators and wavefunctions in a continuous representation.

Formally, we may write a quantum state \(\left\vert \psi \right\rangle\) as a column matrix in the continuous position representation:

\[ \begin{array}{c} \left\vert \psi \right\rangle \buildrel\textstyle.\over= \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} \vdots \\ {\psi (x - \delta x)}\\ {\psi (x)}\\ {\psi (x + \delta x)}\\ \vdots \end{array}} \right)\sqrt {\delta x} {\rm{,}} & \\ \left\langle \psi \right\vert \buildrel\textstyle.\over= \sqrt {\delta x} \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} \cdots &{{\psi ^*}(x - \delta x)}&{{\psi ^*}(x)}&{{\psi ^*}(x + \delta x)}& \cdots \end{array}} \right) \end{array} \tag{11.4}\]

\[ \left\langle {\psi } \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {\psi \psi }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {\psi } \right\rangle = \mathop {\lim }\limits_{\delta x \to 0} \sum\limits_{n = - \infty }^\infty {{{\left\vert {\psi (x + n\delta x)} \right\vert }^2}\delta x} = \int\limits_{ - \infty }^\infty {{{\left\vert {\psi (x)} \right\vert }^2}dx} = 1 \tag{11.5}\]

We can also express momentum as an infinite-dimensional continuous matrix:

\[ \begin{array}{c} - i\hbar \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {}&{}&{}&{}&{}&{}\\ {}& \ddots &{}&{}&{}&{}\\ \cdots &{ - \frac{1}{{2\delta x}}}&0&{\frac{1}{{2\delta x}}}&0& \cdots \\ \cdots &0&{ - \frac{1}{{2\delta x}}}&0&{\frac{1}{{2\delta x}}}& \cdots \\ {}&{}&{}&{}& \ddots &{}\\ {}&{}&{}&{}&{}&{} \end{array}} \right)\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} \vdots \\ {\psi (x - \delta x)}\\ {\psi (x)}\\ {\psi (x + \delta x)}\\ {\psi (x + 2\delta x)}\\ \vdots \end{array}} \right)\\ = - i\hbar \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} \vdots \\ {\frac{{\psi (x + \delta x) - \psi (x - \delta x)}}{{2\delta x}}}\\ {\frac{{\psi (x + 2\delta x) - \psi (x)}}{{2\delta x}}}\\ \vdots \end{array}} \right) = - i\hbar \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} \vdots \\ {{{\left. {\frac{{d\psi }}{{dx}}} \right\vert }_x}}\\ {{{\left. {\frac{{d\psi }}{{dx}}} \right\vert }_{x + \delta x}}}\\ \vdots \end{array}} \right) = - i\hbar \frac{d}{{dx}}\psi (x) \end{array} \tag{11.6}\]

11.4 Poisson Brackets

The classical Poisson bracket \(\left\{ {} \right\}\) is defined by

\[ {\rm{\{ }}F,G\} = \sum\limits_j {\left( {\frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial {q_j}}}\frac{{\partial G}}{{\partial {p_j}}} - \frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial {p_j}}}\frac{{\partial G}}{{\partial {q_j}}}} \right)} \tag{11.11}\]

Here \({q_j},{p_j}\) are the generalized position and momentum of particle \(j\).

The equation of motion of a dynamical quantity \(F\) is

\[ \frac{{dF}}{{dt}} = \left\{ {F,H} \right\} + \frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial t}} \tag{11.12}\]

The quantum commutator corresponding to a classical Poisson bracket is

\[ \left\{ {\;\;\;,\;\;\;} \right\} \to \frac{{[\;\;\;,\;\;\;]}}{{i\hbar }} \tag{11.13}\]

For example,

\[ \left\{ {x,p} \right\} = 1 \to \frac{{[\hat x,\hat p]}}{{i\hbar }} = 1 \tag{11.14}\]

11.5 The Heisenberg Picture

If \(\hat H\) has no explicit time dependence,

\[ \hat U(t) = \exp \left( {\frac{{ - i\hat Ht}}{\hbar }} \right) \tag{11.15}\]

We usually use the Schrödinger picture, in which states evolve with time. In the Heisenberg picture, the state is held fixed:

\[ \vert {\psi (t)}\rangle = \vert {\psi (0)}\rangle = \vert \psi\rangle \tag{11.16}\]

The observables evolve instead:

\[ {\hat A_{\rm{H}}}(t) = {\hat U^\dag }(t){\hat A_{\rm{S}}}\hat U(t) \tag{11.17}\]

Subscripts S and H denote the Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures. From Equation 11.17, clearly \({\hat A_{\rm{H}}}(0) = {\hat A_{\rm{S}}}\), while the energy operator remains constant in the Heisenberg picture: \({\hat H_{\rm{H}}}(t) = {\hat H_{\rm{S}}} = \hat H\).

If \(\hat A\) has no explicit time dependence,

\[ \frac{d}{{dt}}{\hat A_{\rm{H}}} = \frac{1}{{i\hbar }}[{\hat A_{\rm{H}}},\hat H] \tag{11.18}\]

\[ \begin{array}{c} \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert {{{\hat A}_{\rm{H}}}}(t)\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert {{U^\dag }(t){{\hat A}_{\rm{S}}}U(t)}\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle \\ = \exp \left[ {\frac{{i({E_j} - {E_k})}}{\hbar }} \right]\left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert {{{\hat A}_{\rm{H}}}(0)}\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle \end{array} \tag{11.19}\]
