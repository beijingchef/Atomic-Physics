5  Time Evolution

So far we have discussed states at a given instant. Even sequences of measurements were implicitly performed immediately one after another, before appreciable evolution could occur. We now examine how a state changes with time.

Quantum postulate 6. Between measurements, a system’s state evolves according to the Schrödinger equation.

The Schrödinger equation plays the role in quantum mechanics that Newton’s second law plays in classical mechanics. It cannot be deduced from more elementary principles within this presentation; its validity ultimately rests on experiment.

5.1 * Motivating the Schrödinger equation

Consider a modern route to the equation. An initial state \(\vert \psi (0)\rangle\) evolves after time \(t\) into \(\vert \psi (t)\rangle\). We seek the law governing this evolution:

\[ \left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle \xrightarrow{{\text{time evolution}}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.1}\]

Introduce a time-evolution operator \(\hat U(t)\) relating the initial state to that after \(t\):

\[ \hat U\left( t \right)\left\vert {\psi (0)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle \tag{5.2}\]

Physical considerations impose three requirements.

First: as the elapsed time tends to zero, \(\hat U(t)\) leaves the state unchanged:

\[ \mathop {\lim }\limits_{t \to 0} \hat U\left( t \right) = \hat I \tag{5.3}\]

Second: two successive intervals compose to give evolution over the combined interval.

\[ \hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right)\hat U\left( {{t_1}} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle = \hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( {{t_1}} \right)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi \left( {{t_1} + {t_2}} \right)} \right\rangle = \hat U\left( {{t_1} + {t_2}} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.4}\]

Therefore,

\[ \hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right)\hat U\left( {{t_1}} \right) = \hat U\left( {{t_1} + {t_2}} \right) = \hat U\left( {{t_1}} \right)\hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right) \tag{5.5}\]

Third: probability is conserved, \(\langle {\psi (t)}\vert {\psi (t)}\rangle = \langle {\psi (0)}\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle\), so

\[ {\hat U^\dag }\left( t \right)\hat U\left( t \right) = \hat I \tag{5.6}\]

Thus \(\hat U\) is unitary: time evolution is a unitary transformation.

These requirements suggest an infinitesimal evolution operator \(\hat U(dt)\):

\[ \hat U\left( {dt} \right)\left\vert {\psi (0)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi (dt)} \right\rangle \tag{5.7}\]

\[ \hat U\left( {dt} \right) \equiv \hat I - i\hat \Omega dt \tag{5.8}\]

Neglecting higher-order terms in \(dt\), \(\hat U(dt)\) satisfies the first two requirements. The third gives

\[ \left( {\hat I{\rm{ + }}i{{\hat \Omega }^\dag }dt} \right)\left( {\hat I - i\hat \Omega dt} \right) = I \tag{5.9}\]

Expanding and neglecting higher-order terms in \(dt\),

\[ \hat I{\rm{ + }}idt\hat I\left( {{{\hat \Omega }^\dag } - \hat \Omega } \right) = \hat I \tag{5.10}\]

Hence

\[ \hat \Omega = {\hat \Omega ^\dag } \tag{5.11}\]

Thus \(\hat \Omega\) must be Hermitian, suggesting an observable. Equation Equation 5.8 shows that \(\hat \Omega\) has frequency dimensions. Classical frequency describes a rate of variation rather than a fundamental dynamical observable. Planck’s frequency–energy relation, however, gives

\[ E = \hbar \omega \tag{5.12}\]

The energy operator, or Hamiltonian, is denoted \(\hat H\). We therefore propose

\[ \hat \Omega = {{\hat H} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{\hat H} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar } \tag{5.13}\]

The infinitesimal evolution operator becomes

\[ \hat U\left( {dt} \right) = \hat I - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }dt \tag{5.14}\]

Because of the central role of \(\hat H\), energy is called the generator of time evolution.

From \(\hat U(dt)\), we obtain a differential equation for \(\hat U(t)\), defined in Equation 5.2:

\[ \hat U\left( {t + dt} \right) = \hat U\left( {dt} \right)\hat U\left( t \right) = \left( {\hat I - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }dt} \right)\hat U\left( t \right) \tag{5.15}\]

Thus,

\[ \frac{{\hat U\left( {t + dt} \right) - \hat U\left( t \right)}}{{dt}} = - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }\hat U\left( t \right) \tag{5.16}\]

Multiplying by \(i\hbar\) gives

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\hat U\left( t \right) = \hat H\hat U\left( t \right) \tag{5.17}\]

Equation Equation 5.17 is the Schrödinger equation for the evolution operator. Acting with both sides of Equation 5.16 on \(\vert \psi (0)\rangle\) gives the familiar state equation:

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.18}\]

5.2 Solving the Schrödinger equation

Here \(\vert \psi (t)\rangle\) is the state at time \(t\), and \(\hat H\) is the Hamiltonian. The equation describes state evolution and is first order in time, so only the initial condition \(\vert \psi (0)\rangle\) is needed. Knowing the Hamiltonian and the state at one instant determines all subsequent states.

Assume \(\hat H\) has no explicit time dependence. Then \(\hat H\) is constant in time, and \(\hat H\) has fixed eigenvalues \({E_j}\) and eigenvectors \(\vert {E_j}\rangle\):

\[ \hat H\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle = {E_j}\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \tag{5.19}\]

The energy eigenvalue equation is also called the time-independent Schrödinger equation. Energy is central to quantum mechanics: many problems reduce to constructing the Hamiltonian and finding its eigenvalues and eigenstates exactly or approximately.

Since the Schrödinger equation contains \(\hat H\), the \(\hat H\) representation is convenient. Multiply Equation 5.18 on the left by \(\langle {E_j}\vert\):

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = {E_j}\left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle \tag{5.20}\]

This is a first-order differential equation for \(\langle {E_j}\vert \psi (t)\rangle\), with solution

\[ \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}} \tag{5.21}\]

Hence the energy-basis expansion of \(\vert \psi (t)\rangle\) is

\[ \left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle } = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \tag{5.22}\]

This is the general solution for time-independent \(\hat H\). Comparing the initial and final expansions makes the result easy to remember.

\[ \begin{array}{c} \left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle } \\ \left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \end{array} \tag{5.23}\]

5.2.1 Stationary states

Suppose the initial state is an eigenstate of \(\hat H\):

\[ \left\vert {\psi {\rm{(}}0)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {{E_m}} \right\rangle \tag{5.24}\]

Then Equation 5.22 becomes

\[ \begin{array}{c} \left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle {\delta _{jm}}{e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} = \left\vert {{E_m}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_m}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_m}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}\\ = {e^{ - {{i{E_m}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_m}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}\left\vert {\psi (0)} \right\rangle \end{array} \tag{5.25}\]

The final vector differs from the initial one only by an overall phase, so they represent the same physical state. For a time-independent Hamiltonian, an initial energy eigenstate therefore remains physically unchanged and is called a stationary state. Since a state contains all information about the system, its time-independent physical properties remain unchanged.

5.3 Time evolution of expectation values

Once the state evolution is known, the expectation value of any observable \(\hat A\) follows from \(\langle {\hat A(t)}\rangle \equiv \left\langle {\psi (t)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle\). Use Equation 5.23 to obtain \(\left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle\), then calculate \(\left\langle {\psi (t)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle\).

More generally, Equation 3.5 and Equation 5.23 give

\[ \begin{array}{c} \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \sum\limits_{j,k} {{e^{{{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}\left\langle {{\psi (0)}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{\psi (0)} {{E_j}}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{{E_j}}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_k}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_k}} {\psi (0)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi (0)}} \right\rangle {e^{{{ - i{E_k}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{ - i{E_k}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \\ = \sum\limits_{j,k} {\left\langle {{\psi (0)}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{\psi (0)} {{E_j}}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{{E_j}}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_k}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_k}} {\psi (0)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi (0)}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle {e^{{{i\left( {{E_j} - {E_k}} \right)t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i\left( {{E_j} - {E_k}} \right)t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \end{array} \tag{5.26}\]

Without the final time-dependent phase in each summand, this is the expectation of \(\hat A\) in initial state \(\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle\). If the initial state contains components \(\vert {E_j}\rangle\) and \(\vert {E_k}\rangle\) of different energies, with \(\left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi (0)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi (0)}} \right\rangle\) and \(\left\langle {{{E_k}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_k}} {\psi (0)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi (0)}} \right\rangle\) both nonzero, and \(\hat A\) has nonzero matrix element \({A_{jk}} \equiv \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle \ne 0\) between them, the mean of \(\hat A\) contains an oscillation at frequency \({\omega _{jk}} \equiv {{({E_j} - {E_k})} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{({E_j} - {E_k})} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }\). This offers a qualitative picture of atomic radiation: take \(\hat A\) to be electron position. If a perturbation mixes energy state \(\vert {E_k}\rangle\) into an initial eigenstate \(\vert {E_j}\rangle\), the mean position oscillates at \({\omega _{jk}}\), suggesting radiation at that frequency during a transition. Heisenberg arrived at Equation 5.26 while studying transitions and built matrix mechanics around this insight. The matrix form of Equation 5.26 is

\[ \left\langle {\psi (t)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle \buildrel\textstyle.\over= {\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\langle 1\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ {\langle 2\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ \vdots \\ \vdots \end{array}} \right)^\dag }\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {{A_{11}}}&{{A_{12}}{e^{i{\omega _{12}}t}}}&{{A_{13}}{e^{{\omega _{13}}t}}}& \cdots \\ {{A_{21}}{e^{i{\omega _{21}}t}}}&{{A_{22}}}&{{A_{23}}{e^{{\omega _{23}}t}}}& \cdots \\ {{A_{31}}{e^{i{\omega _{31}}t}}}&{{A_{32}}{e^{{\omega _{32}}t}}}&{{A_{33}}}& \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{array}} \right)\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\langle 1\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ {\langle 2\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ \vdots \\ \vdots \end{array}} \right) \tag{5.27}\]

5.4 Constants of motion

If \(\hat A\) has no explicit time dependence, there is a direct way to find the evolution of \(\left\langle {\psi (t)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle\).

First write the Schrödinger equation in the bra space:

\[ - i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert = \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat H \tag{5.28}\]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{array}{c} \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \left( {\frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert } \right)\hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \\ = \frac{{ - 1}}{{i\hbar }}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat H\hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle + \frac{1}{{i\hbar }}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \end{array} \tag{5.29}\]

Multiply by \(i\hbar\) to obtain

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert [\hat A,\hat H]\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.30}\]

This is often abbreviated as

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\langle \hat A(t)\rangle = \langle [\hat A,\hat H]\rangle \tag{5.31}\]

By Equation 5.30, if \(\hat A\) has no explicit time dependence and commutes with the Hamiltonian,

\[ \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = 0 \tag{5.32}\]

The mean of \(\hat A\) is constant. If \(\hat A\) initially has a definite value, that value equals its mean and \(\hat A\) retains it.

An observable with no explicit time dependence that commutes with the Hamiltonian is a constant of motion. Its mean is constant, and any initially definite value remains definite and unchanged. Quantum numbers labeling conserved observables are called good quantum numbers.

Whether a quantity is conserved depends on the system’s Hamiltonian. An operator always commutes with itself, so energy is conserved for the time-independent Hamiltonians considered here.

5.5 Exercises

5.1. An electron has magnetic moment \({\bf{\mu }} = {{ - 2{\mu _{\rm{B}}}{\bf{S}}} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{ - 2{\mu _{\rm{B}}}{\bf{S}}} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }\) and magnetic energy \(H = - {\bf{\mu }} \cdot {\bf{B}}\). Place a stationary electron in a static field \({\bf{B}} = B{\bf{\hat z}}\).

  1. Given the initial condition \({S_z} = - {\hbar \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {\hbar 2}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } 2}\), find \(\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle\).

  2. Let \({\bf{\hat n}}\) be a unit vector in the first quadrant of the \(x\)–\(z\) plane, making angle \(\theta\) with \(z\). Initially the projection of \({\bf{S}}\) along \({\bf{\hat n}}\) is \({\hbar \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {\hbar 2}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } 2}\). Using the preceding chapter’s exercises, find spin state \(\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle\) at time \(t\) and the expectation value of \({\hat S_x},\;{\hat S_y},\;{\hat S_z}\). You may define \({\omega _L} \equiv {{2{\mu _{\rm{B}}}B} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{2{\mu _{\rm{B}}}B} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }\) to simplify the result.
