5  Time Evolution

Imagine preparing an electron’s spin, then waiting before you measure it. Will you get the same result as before? So far, we have treated measurements as snapshots, or taken them one after another with almost no delay. Now we let the clock run and ask how the state changes.

Quantum postulate 6. Between measurements, a system’s state evolves according to the Schrödinger equation.

Newton’s second law tells us how a classical system moves. The Schrödinger equation does the same job for a quantum state. Here we take it as a basic law, supported by experiment. Before solving it, let us explore why it has the form it does.

5.1 * Motivating the Schrödinger equation

Start with a state \(\vert \psi (0)\rangle\). After a time \(t\), it becomes \(\vert \psi (t)\rangle\). We want a rule that connects these two states:

\[ \left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle \xrightarrow{{\text{time evolution}}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.1}\]

We use an operator to describe this change. Call it the time-evolution operator, \(\hat U(t)\):

\[ \hat U\left( t \right)\left\vert {\psi (0)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle \tag{5.2}\]

What should this operator do? Three simple requirements guide us.

First: if no time passes, the state must stay the same:

\[ \mathop {\lim }\limits_{t \to 0} \hat U\left( t \right) = \hat I \tag{5.3}\]

Second: waiting through two successive intervals must give the same result as waiting through their sum. Here we assume that the evolution law does not itself change with time.

\[ \hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right)\hat U\left( {{t_1}} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle = \hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( {{t_1}} \right)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi \left( {{t_1} + {t_2}} \right)} \right\rangle = \hat U\left( {{t_1} + {t_2}} \right)\left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.4}\]

Therefore,

\[ \hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right)\hat U\left( {{t_1}} \right) = \hat U\left( {{t_1} + {t_2}} \right) = \hat U\left( {{t_1}} \right)\hat U\left( {{t_2}} \right) \tag{5.5}\]

Third: the total probability must stay equal to one. In symbols, \(\langle {\psi (t)}\vert {\psi (t)}\rangle = \langle {\psi (0)}\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle\), so

\[ {\hat U^\dagger }\left( t \right)\hat U\left( t \right) = \hat I \tag{5.6}\]

This makes \(\hat U\) unitary. Time evolution preserves the length of a state vector.

For a tiny time step \(dt\), these requirements suggest

\[ \hat U\left( {dt} \right)\left\vert {\psi (0)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {\psi (dt)} \right\rangle \tag{5.7}\]

where

\[ \hat U\left( {dt} \right) \equiv \hat I - i\hat \Omega dt \tag{5.8}\]

To first order in \(dt\), this expression satisfies the first two requirements. The probability requirement gives

\[ \left( {\hat I{\rm{ + }}i{{\hat \Omega }^\dagger }dt} \right)\left( {\hat I - i\hat \Omega dt} \right) = I \tag{5.9}\]

Expand the product and keep only terms through first order in \(dt\):

\[ \hat I{\rm{ + }}idt\hat I\left( {{{\hat \Omega }^\dagger } - \hat \Omega } \right) = \hat I \tag{5.10}\]

Hence

\[ \hat \Omega = {\hat \Omega ^\dagger } \tag{5.11}\]

So \(\hat \Omega\) is Hermitian, just as an observable’s operator should be. Its units also give us a clue: by Equation 5.8, \(\hat \Omega\) has units of frequency. Planck’s relation connects frequency to energy:

\[ E = \hbar \omega \tag{5.12}\]

We call the energy operator the Hamiltonian and write it as \(\hat H\). This suggests the identification

\[ \hat \Omega = {{\hat H} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{\hat H} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar } \tag{5.13}\]

The infinitesimal evolution operator becomes

\[ \hat U\left( {dt} \right) = \hat I - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }dt \tag{5.14}\]

The Hamiltonian tells the state how to change at each tiny time step. For this reason, we call it the generator of time evolution.

let both sides of Equation 5.14 act on \(\vert \psi (t)\rangle\):

\[ \vert \psi (t+dt)\rangle = \vert \psi (t)\rangle - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }dt\vert \psi (t)\rangle \]

Thus,

\[ \frac{\vert \psi (t+dt)\rangle - \vert \psi (t)\rangle}{{dt}} = - i\frac{{\hat H}}{\hbar }\vert \psi (t)\rangle \tag{5.15}\]

Multiplying by \(i\hbar\) gives

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.16}\]

5.2 Solving the Schrödinger equation

Here \(\vert \psi (t)\rangle\) is the state at time \(t\), and \(\hat H\) is the Hamiltonian. Notice that the equation contains only a first derivative with respect to time. We therefore need just one starting state, \(\vert \psi (0)\rangle\). Once we know that state and the Hamiltonian, the equation tells us what happens next.

Let us start with a Hamiltonian that has no explicit time dependence. Its energy eigenvalues \(E_j\) and eigenvectors \(\vert E_j\rangle\) are then fixed:

\[ \hat H\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle = {E_j}\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \tag{5.17}\]

We call this eigenvalue equation the time-independent Schrödinger equation. It appears again and again in quantum mechanics. In many problems, our main task is to write down the Hamiltonian and find its energy eigenstates, either exactly or approximately.

The energy basis makes the next step easier because \(\hat H\) acts so simply on each basis vector. Multiply Equation 5.16 on the left by \(\langle E_j\vert\):

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = {E_j}\left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle \tag{5.18}\]

Each amplitude \(\langle E_j\vert\psi(t)\rangle\) now obeys its own first-order differential equation. Its solution is

\[ \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle = \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}} \tag{5.19}\]

Put these amplitudes back into the energy-basis expansion:

\[ \left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( t \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( t \right)}} \right\rangle } = \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \tag{5.20}\]

This solves the problem for a time-independent Hamiltonian. The rule is simple: expand the initial state in energy eigenstates, then multiply each component by its own phase factor. Compare the two expansions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\vert {\psi \left( 0 \right)} \right\rangle &= \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle } \\ \left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle &= \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_j}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_j}} {\psi \left( 0 \right)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi \left( 0 \right)}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \end{aligned} \tag{5.21}\]

5.2.1 Stationary states

What happens if we start with just one energy eigenstate? Suppose

\[ \left\vert {\psi {\rm{(}}0)} \right\rangle = \left\vert {{E_m}} \right\rangle \tag{5.22}\]

Then Equation 5.20 becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle &= \sum\limits_j {\left\vert {{E_j}} \right\rangle {\delta _{jm}}{e^{ - {{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} = \left\vert {{E_m}} \right\rangle {e^{ - {{i{E_m}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_m}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}} \\ &= {e^{ - {{i{E_m}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_m}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}\left\vert {\psi (0)} \right\rangle \end{aligned} \tag{5.23}\]

Only an overall phase has changed. As we learned earlier, that phase does not change the physical state. We call an energy eigenstate of a time-independent Hamiltonian a stationary state. Its measurement probabilities stay the same, even though its ket acquires a time-dependent phase.

5.3 Time evolution of expectation values

Once we know the state, we can calculate an observable’s expectation value. For \(\hat A\), it is \(\langle\hat A(t)\rangle\equiv\langle\psi(t)\vert\hat A\vert\psi(t)\rangle\). Use Equation 5.21 to evolve the ket, take its adjoint to get the bra, and place \(\hat A\) between them.

Using Equation 3.5 and Equation 5.21, we get

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle &= \sum\limits_{j,k} {{e^{{{i{E_j}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i{E_j}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}\left\langle {{\psi (0)}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{\psi (0)} {{E_j}}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{{E_j}}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_k}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_k}} {\psi (0)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi (0)}} \right\rangle {e^{{{ - i{E_k}t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{ - i{E_k}t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \\ &= \sum\limits_{j,k} {\left\langle {{\psi (0)}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{\psi (0)} {{E_j}}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{{E_j}}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{{E_k}}} \mathrel{\left \vert {\vphantom {{{E_k}} {\psi (0)}}} \right. \kern-1.2pt } {{\psi (0)}} \right\rangle \left\langle {{E_j}} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {{E_k}} \right\rangle {e^{{{i\left( {{E_j} - {E_k}} \right)t} \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{i\left( {{E_j} - {E_k}} \right)t} \hbar }} \right. \kern-1.2pt } \hbar }}}} \end{aligned} \tag{5.24}\]

Look at the phase factor at the end of each term. At \(t=0\), it is one, and the expression gives the initial expectation value. As time passes, components with different energies acquire different phases. These relative phases can make the expectation value oscillate.

For a particular pair of energy states, both initial amplitudes must be nonzero. The matrix element \(A_{jk}\equiv\langle E_j\vert\hat A\vert E_k\rangle\) must also be nonzero. That pair then contributes a term with angular frequency \(\omega_{jk}=(E_j-E_k)/\hbar\).

To picture the connection with atomic radiation, take \(\hat A\) to be the electron’s position. A perturbation can mix two energy states, making the mean position oscillate at their energy-difference frequency. This gives useful intuition for the frequency of a spectral line. It is not a full account of spontaneous emission. Heisenberg used the link between transition frequencies and matrix elements to develop matrix mechanics. We can write Equation 5.24 in matrix form as

\[ \left\langle {\psi (t)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi (t)} \right\rangle \buildrel\textstyle.\over= {\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\langle 1\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ {\langle 2\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ \vdots \\ \vdots \end{array}} \right)^\dagger }\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {{A_{11}}}&{{A_{12}}{e^{i{\omega _{12}}t}}}&{{A_{13}}{e^{{\omega _{13}}t}}}& \cdots \\ {{A_{21}}{e^{i{\omega _{21}}t}}}&{{A_{22}}}&{{A_{23}}{e^{{\omega _{23}}t}}}& \cdots \\ {{A_{31}}{e^{i{\omega _{31}}t}}}&{{A_{32}}{e^{{\omega _{32}}t}}}&{{A_{33}}}& \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{array}} \right)\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\langle 1\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ {\langle 2\vert {\psi (0)}\rangle }\\ \vdots \\ \vdots \end{array}} \right) \tag{5.25}\]

We can also find how an expectation value changes without solving for the whole state first. Assume that \(\hat A\) has no explicit time dependence.

First, take the adjoint of the Schrödinger equation to get its bra form:

\[ - i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert = \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat H \tag{5.26}\]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left(\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle\right) &= \left( {\frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert } \right)\hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle + \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A \left(\frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \right) \\ &= \frac{{ - 1}}{{i\hbar }}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat H\hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle + \frac{1}{{i\hbar }}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\hat H\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \end{aligned} \tag{5.27}\]

Multiply by \(i\hbar\) to obtain

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = \left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert [\hat A,\hat H]\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle \tag{5.28}\]

This is often abbreviated as

\[ i\hbar \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\langle \hat A(t)\rangle = \langle [\hat A,\hat H]\rangle \tag{5.29}\]

5.4 Constants of motion

Now suppose \(\hat A\) has no explicit time dependence and commutes with the Hamiltonian: \([\hat A,\hat H]=0\). Equation 5.28 then gives

\[ \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}\left\langle {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\vert \hat A\left\vert {\psi \left( t \right)} \right\rangle = 0 \tag{5.30}\]

The expectation value stays constant. There is an even stronger result: if \(\hat A\) starts with a definite value, it keeps that value. Exercise 5.2 explores why the whole probability distribution stays fixed.

We call such an observable a constant of motion, or a conserved observable. Its quantum numbers are good quantum numbers: we can use them to label the state throughout the evolution. Energy provides a familiar example. Since \([\hat H,\hat H]=0\), energy is conserved when the Hamiltonian has no explicit time dependence.

5.5 Exercises

5.1. Place an electron at rest in a static magnetic field \(\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}\). Its magnetic moment is \(\boldsymbol\mu=-2\mu_{\mathrm B}\mathbf S/\hbar\), and its magnetic energy is \(H=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B\).

  1. Start with \(S_z=-\hbar/2\). Find \(\vert\psi(t)\rangle\).

  2. Choose a unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) in the first quadrant of the \(x\)–\(z\) plane. It makes an angle \(\theta\) with the \(z\) axis. Initially, the spin component along \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) is \(\hbar/2\). Use the preceding chapter’s exercises to find \(\vert\psi(t)\rangle\) and the expectation values of \(\hat S_x\), \(\hat S_y\), and \(\hat S_z\). You may write \(\omega_L\equiv2\mu_{\mathrm B}B/\hbar\) to simplify your answer.

5.2.* Suppose \(\hat A\) is conserved and has a discrete spectrum. Show that its entire measurement probability distribution stays constant.

Hint: let \(\hat P_j\) project onto the eigenspace with eigenvalue \(a_j\). First show that \(\hat P_j\) is conserved. Then use its expectation value to find the probability of measuring \(a_j\).
